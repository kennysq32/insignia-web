An **insignia** is a glyph of light, broadcast from a [[pylon]], that binds a name to the person or place that receives it. For most of recorded history, to bear an insignia was to be *remembered*: property, kinship and debt were kept in light rather than in ink.[^book] Since [[the Dimming]] no new insignia have been broadcast, and the surviving ones are slowly fading from the people who carry them.

## Etymology

The word is borrowed from an older ceremonial language in which it meant "that which is set upon". Early guild records use it only for the glyphs set upon *places* — a hill, a well, a crossing — and it is only from around 400 BD that it is used for people.

## Description

An insignia is composed on a grid of seven by seven points. Each point is either lit or dark, and every glyph is mirror-symmetric about its vertical axis, which leaves 28 meaningful points and a little over 268 million possible glyphs. The centre point is always lit. Readers call it the *hearth*.

![The Dimming, as a halftone study](/images/art/dimming.svg "Halftone study of a fading field of insignia, after a guild print")

### Reading

Reading an insignia is less like reading a word than like recognising a face. Trained readers of the [[Lantern Guild]] describe the glyph "arriving all at once". Untrained people see only a pattern of dots, though most can tell their own insignia from any other at a glance.[^varo]

### Carrying

A received insignia persists as a faint afterimage that the bearer can recall at will, and that others can read when it is recalled in front of them. It is not visible in ordinary light.

## Loss

After [[the Dimming]], insignia began to fade from their bearers at a rate of roughly one point per generation. The first point to fade is never the hearth. Families in [[Halden]] keep painted copies of their insignia on the lintels of their doors so that children can learn the full glyph before their own copy thins.

## In culture

Insignia appear on doors, in lullabies and in the grammar of the city. "To go dark" is to be forgotten; "an unlit name" is a polite phrase for a stranger. The seven-point grid is also the basis of most of the region's textile patterns.
